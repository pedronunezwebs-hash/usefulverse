Effect Size Calculator

Enter summary statistics for two independent groups to calculate their signed standardized mean difference, pooled standard deviation and small-sample corrected Hedges’ g.

For independent groups, this version uses the conventional pooled-SD definition and assumes a shared population variance. Enter sample (n−1) standard deviations, not standard errors.

Each sample size must be 2–1,000,000; means and SDs are limited to ±1,000,000,000 (SDs cannot be negative). At least one SD must be above zero.

Effect size results

d = (mean₁ − mean₂) / pooled SD. Hedges’ g = J(df) × d, with df = n₁ + n₂ − 2. The sign depends on group order. A magnitude is not automatically “small”, “medium” or “large”; interpret it in the subject area and study design.

What does an effect size calculator measure?

It expresses the difference between two group means in pooled standard-deviation units. Cohen’s d is the uncorrected standardized difference; Hedges’ g applies a small-sample correction.

Frequently asked questions

What does an effect size calculator measure?

It expresses the difference between two group means in pooled standard-deviation units. Cohen’s d is the uncorrected standardized difference; Hedges’ g applies a small-sample correction.

Are my study statistics uploaded?

No. The calculation runs in this browser. Your summary statistics are not sent to a server or saved by this tool.

What is the difference between Cohen’s d and Hedges’ g?

Both standardize the mean difference by a pooled standard deviation. Hedges’ g multiplies d by a small-sample correction, which reduces bias when samples are small.

Can I enter standard errors instead of standard deviations?

No. This calculator expects each group’s sample standard deviation. A standard error describes uncertainty in a sample estimate and is not interchangeable with the SD here.

Does a larger effect size prove that a result is important?

No. Effect size describes a standardized difference, not statistical significance, causation or practical importance. Context, uncertainty, study design and the measurement scale matter.
